_The Emotional Brain · Part 2 of 4._

Fear tells us to be careful.

Anger tells us that something needs to change.

Sometimes they work together. Sometimes one seems to overpower the other. And sometimes we become so accustomed to either fighting or avoiding that we forget these are supposed to be responses to particular situations, not permanent ways of living.

In Jaak Panksepp's model of seven primary emotional systems, **RAGE and FEAR** are two ancient systems involved in responding to threats.

Both can protect us.

Both can become difficult to regulate.

And neither is inherently a sign of psychological weakness.

### 3. RAGE: The Energy to Fight Back

Imagine someone repeatedly crossing your boundaries.

They ignore your objections, dismiss your needs, or prevent you from doing something important.

At some point, irritation turns into anger.

Your attention narrows. Your body becomes mobilised. You want to push back, confront the obstacle, or make the situation stop.

This is the kind of response Panksepp associated with the RAGE system.

In animal research, circuits involving the medial amygdala, hypothalamus, and periaqueductal gray (PAG) have been implicated in anger-related defensive behaviour.

Glutamate contributes to excitatory signalling, while GABA helps regulate neural activity. Substance P is among the neuropeptides associated with anger-related circuits.

Serotonin, vasopressin, and hormones such as testosterone can also influence aggression-related behaviour, although their effects are complex and depend heavily on context.

There is no single rage chemical.

And no single brain region that turns an otherwise peaceful person into an aggressor.

**When RAGE Works Well**

Anger can tell us that something matters.

A boundary has been crossed. A situation feels unjust. Something we value is being threatened.

Healthy anger doesn't require shouting, intimidation, or violence.

Sometimes it means having a difficult conversation.

Sometimes it means refusing an unreasonable demand.

Sometimes it simply means saying _no_ and accepting that the other person may not like it.

Anger can provide the energy for self-protection without determining how that energy must be expressed.

**When RAGE Runs Too Hot**

Now imagine that nearly everything feels like an attack.

A disagreement becomes disrespect. A minor inconvenience becomes an insult. A colleague's mistake becomes evidence of hostility.

Life turns into a series of battles.

When anger is intense, persistent, or poorly regulated, it can contribute to impulsive aggression and destructive conflicts.

But anger and aggression are not the same thing.

People can feel intense anger without harming anyone. And some harmful or violent behaviour is deliberate rather than driven by rage.

Having a powerful RAGE response does not automatically explain domestic violence, criminal behaviour, or a personality disorder.

Those behaviours involve additional psychological, social, and contextual factors.

**When RAGE Has No Voice**

The other extreme can be less visible.

Some people have learned that expressing anger is dangerous.

Perhaps anger was punished in childhood. Perhaps disagreement threatened an important relationship. Perhaps being a good person came to mean never becoming angry.

So they accommodate.

They apologise. They tolerate things they resent. They say yes when they desperately want to say no.

The anger may remain difficult to recognise or express.

This can contribute to resentment, distress, and difficulty maintaining boundaries.

But it would be misleading to claim that suppressed anger automatically causes migraines, depression, or self-harm. These experiences are complex and cannot be explained by a single emotional system.

The important distinction is that **not expressing anger is not necessarily the same as regulating it.**

Sometimes it simply means losing access to an important signal about what we need.

### 4. FEAR: The Intelligence of Caution

Fear has a rather unfortunate reputation.

We admire courage, confidence, and risk-taking. We tell people to stop being afraid, as though fear were an embarrassing defect in the design of human beings.

Yet without fear, survival would become considerably more difficult.

In Panksepp's framework, the FEAR system helps organise responses to danger.

Its neural circuitry involves the amygdala, hypothalamus, and periaqueductal gray, interacting with wider networks responsible for attention, memory, and physiological arousal.

Noradrenaline contributes to alertness and vigilance. Glutamate and GABA participate in excitatory and inhibitory signalling.

Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) helps coordinate stress responses, including activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and subsequent cortisol release.

These processes prepare us to respond to threats, but they do not operate as a single on-off switch.

**When FEAR Works Well**

Healthy fear is not cowardice.

It's information.

It encourages us to notice risks, anticipate consequences, and avoid situations that could cause harm.

It can stop us from crossing a dangerous road, trusting an obviously unreliable person, or making a decision whose potential consequences we haven't considered.

Fear can also motivate preparation.

We check the details. We make a plan. We ask questions.

The goal isn't to eliminate uncertainty.

It's to respond to uncertainty without becoming paralysed by it.

**When FEAR Runs Too Hot**

Now imagine a threat-detection system that rarely settles down.

Every unfamiliar situation suggests danger.

Every ambiguous message might contain rejection.

Every new opportunity comes with an elaborate catalogue of possible disasters.

The person isn't necessarily responding to an immediate threat. Their nervous system may have become unusually sensitive to the possibility of one.

Persistent threat sensitivity can contribute to anxiety, avoidance, hypervigilance, and exhaustion.

Anxiety disorders involve much more than simply having an overactive FEAR system, but altered threat processing is an important part of their study.

The practical consequence can be a shrinking life.

You avoid one uncomfortable situation, then another, then another.

Avoidance brings temporary relief. But it can also prevent you from discovering that some situations are safer or more manageable than expected.

Eventually, the world may appear more dangerous than it actually is.

**When FEAR Falls Quiet**

The opposite isn't automatically courage.

Someone who underestimates danger may take risks without adequately considering the consequences.

They may overlook warning signs, repeatedly enter unsafe situations, or fail to learn from previous experiences.

But risk-taking has many possible explanations, including sensation seeking, impulsivity, learning, and social context.

And the popular equation of low fear with psychopathy is far too simplistic.

Research suggests that some psychopathic traits are associated with differences in threat detection and responding, but not necessarily with an inability to experience fear.

Fearlessness alone is not a diagnosis.

### Two Systems, One Difficult Balance

RAGE and FEAR can pull behaviour in different directions.

Fear may encourage withdrawal. Anger may encourage confrontation.

But real emotional life is rarely that tidy.

A frightened person may become aggressive when feeling trapped.

Someone who is angry may retreat because confrontation feels too dangerous.

And a person who has learned to suppress both emotions may struggle to recognise when they need protection at all.

These systems interact with experience, memory, social learning, and the brain's broader regulatory networks.

Neither exists in isolation.

The challenge isn't to maximise anger or minimise fear.

It's to recognise what each emotion is telling us without allowing it to make every decision.

**Anger can help us defend our boundaries. Fear can help us recognise danger.**

But healthy emotional functioning also requires knowing when to act, when to step away, and when the situation is no longer a threat.

Because the purpose of these ancient systems isn't to keep us permanently ready for battle.

It's to help us live long enough, and freely enough, that we don't have to fight every day.
