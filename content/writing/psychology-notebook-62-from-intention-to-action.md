_A thematic selection of translated notebook fragments._

_Cover image supplied from the owner-authorized Psychology Notebook reserve._

There is an idea that, to take ownership of our achievements, we need bigger and better achievements. The bad news: if you cannot claim the small results as your own, the big ones will be difficult too—and you will have spent an enormous amount of energy.

---

When people ask me, ‘So what should I do?’, expecting a clear instruction, I usually say, ‘Just observe.’ Interestingly, this has a greater effect than ‘Do this, then do that.’ Observation allows attention to return to places where you have become used to acting automatically. That, in turn, is a good beginning for major changes.

---

The appealing idea about the power of thought—that thinking hard enough will get you everything—ignores the fact that any action has three stages: planning, acting and evaluating the result. Action without planning does not produce the desired result. Planning and action without evaluation offer no chance to correct mistakes. But planning and dreaming without action definitely produce no result.

There is a good Ukrainian proverb: ‘A fool grows rich in thought.’

---

Desires need balance and an assessment of whether they can be realized. Desires alone, without consistent action and effort, lead to a dead end and frustration. You imagine yourself owning factories, newspapers and steamships, while your actual salaried position is sofa driver.

Action without desire leads to apathy and a lack of joy or pleasure in life. Without desire, you live as if you were economizing on life itself.

---

Taking ownership of your skills and moving from study to putting them into practice can be blocked by a fear of responsibility: something is expected of a person who knows how. While I have not claimed a skill as my own, I can remain unmanifested and potentially be anyone.

---

Over the long term, passivity often costs more than activity.

---

The pattern of not finishing what you start—dropping your effort just before the end—arises from a fear of definition. If I finish something, I am no longer potentially anyone: I am someone specific and definite. As unfinished tasks and projects accumulate, adaptability declines. First, there is nothing to claim as an achievement; second, there is the way other people respond.

---

When you keep picking on yourself, things may go badly not because you are insufficiently beautiful, intelligent or pleasant, but because you spend your time beating yourself up instead of getting things done.

---

In evolution, something that began as an error or malfunction can become an adaptive advantage when the environment changes. Something similar happens within a human life: what seemed to be a bug may turn out to be a feature.

---

What can you do if you are not suffering? Create and contemplate. When the familiar cycle of drama becomes less enthralling, you realize that looking at leaves on trees, watching clouds, learning something new, improving at what you do and spending time with people who bring joy rather than problems and losses are much more engaging than ‘And then he/she said this to me, and I said that.’
