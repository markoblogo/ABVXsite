_A thematic selection of translated notebook fragments._

_Cover image supplied from the owner-authorized Psychology Notebook reserve._

Have you seen a cat?

When conditions are okay, a cat is usually relaxed. But if something happens that might threaten it—a terrifying vacuum cleaner, say, or a newcomer entering the house—the cat instantly becomes alert and responds to what is happening.

Now imagine a cat that, instead of blissfully lazing around, sits on guard, thinking ahead about what bad things might happen. Imagining that vacuum-cleaner monster, for example. Brrr. There you go.

So today, if you start playing that familiar record of worries, remember the cat on guard.

---

Pseudo-spiritual movements have spoiled New Agers with the idea that following simple practice instructions will change everything.

On one hand, yes, something might change. How you feel, for instance. Breathing, training attention and exercising are all wonderful and useful. Eating healthy, tasty food, living moderately, being active, not fretting over the superstructure but looking at the foundations—all good things to do.

But hoping that morning exercises will resolve your inner conflicts, or even make you a different, better person, is more about infantile idealization than reality.

---

A manipulator provokes emotions not because they are cunning and demonic, but because their inner economy is disrupted. To feel even a shadow of experience, they have to devise elaborate drama when they could simply rest.

Manipulation is usually a sign of immaturity. For a regulated adult, manipulation is tiring: you can make an agreement, and where that is impossible, it is better not to get involved.

---

Judging others and moralizing as expressions of an inner lack of safety. If people do not act according to rules and regulations, they pose a threat that you do not know how to handle.

---

A greedy person, tensely watching for anything they might lose or miss, often misses opportunities to gain something because of that tension. The drama of holding on inevitably leads to a dreary life.

---

Supporting change and supporting someone so they can keep going without changing anything are different kinds of support.

Change always causes anxiety. What matters here is not ‘Come on, you can do anything’—because they might not be able to—but acknowledging that this anxiety makes sense, and being present alongside it.

Supporting a choice to leave everything as it is resembles liberals observing someone drowning: ‘We recognize your right to make your own choice, buddy. How is the water?’

---

A person's range of interests and the scope of their personality are visible in the subjects they speak about. It is rare for someone to talk about something personal and uninteresting while abysses and the cosmos lie within.

---

Ignoring financial self-sufficiency and shifting economic responsibility to someone else is a wish to remain in a child's position: I am a sweet little baby and the adults serve me; the world is too complicated for me, so let other people deal with it.

Sometimes you may manage to secure capable adults for yourself. But the world is such that an infant can become rather tiring. Something can also happen to those adults, and then you have to grow up very quickly or break under the challenges.

---

Secondary stress-related blockage occurs when, instead of thinking constructively about a problem and seeing the whole situation, we split the problem into numerous fragments and give every fragment the status of a separate problem. Thinking then loses its flexibility, a creative approach disappears, and the mind gets caught in loops.

---

Sometimes a serious inner conflict lies in wanting to live as everyone else does, while receiving from life as though you were the author of your own life.
