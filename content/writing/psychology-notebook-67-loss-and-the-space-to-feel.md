_A thematic selection of translated notebook fragments._

_Cover image supplied from the owner-authorized Psychology Notebook reserve._

Sarcasm is laughter at tragedy. This response to another person's pain is deeply defensive and forms around a core of intense pain of one's own.

---

There are four inevitabilities in life: death, freedom, meaninglessness and solitude. Entire social institutions—religion, marriage, science—exist around them.

We cannot overcome these inevitabilities, but we can build an inner relationship with them. Do not run from death towards death: you will die anyway. Do not fill your surroundings with unsuitable people merely to avoid being alone. Recognize where freedom of choice exists. Do not pester the world because it fails to deliver the meaning of life in a box with a bow: here, here, settle down, everything just as you wanted.

---

The right to emotions.

Of course a person has the right to experience anything, in any way. Rage may be proportionate to a threat. Hurt may reflect the severity of the harm done. Sadness may equal the significance of a loss. Despair may reflect the perceived lack of a way out. Emotions carry information and help represent the outside world.

The problem is that emotions cost energy. If they constantly seize someone and whirl them through a storm of passions, a question of inner economics arises: where will the energy for these endless storms come from?

---

Everyone has their own death.

---

Guilt and blame can be important parts of grieving. But when guilt loops without moving on, that means being stuck in bargaining. If it were not for those responsible, everything would have been different—perhaps it would. Does it matter that those responsible should be punished? If that is possible, of course it matters. But endlessly replaying accusations and being consumed by that cycle do not allow grief to be lived through.

---

The fear that people might think something wrong about you is a wish that they should think something about you at all.

---

The more difficult a situation requiring adaptation, the more we need to shorten our time horizon. A threatened loss or a loss that has already occurred is not the time to revisit the past or make plans for the future. This frees up space for immediate adaptation.

---

Experiencing the death of someone close is like finding yourself before a wide-open window on a freezing day—except that the window opens onto the desert of the real. Living through a death does not mean forgetting. It means preserving memory and connection with the person who is no longer here, so that you can return to life. Grieving is necessary so that you do not follow the person who has gone.

---

When inner pain subsides, first, a person begins remembering the pleasant, sustaining things that helped them survive and cope where doing so was extremely difficult. Second, once they are no longer experiencing endless pain, they begin asking important questions about the meaning of their life and how to help other people cope and live through difficulties.

---

On a neutral position.

Someone who is losing what is precious and important to them and says, ‘I am neutral about this’—unless they are a Buddhist one step away from nirvana—has difficulties in their emotional life.

But if you ask for neutrality from someone who is grieving and losing what is precious to them, the emotional difficulties are yours.
