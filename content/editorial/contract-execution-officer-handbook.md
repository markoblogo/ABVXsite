# The Contract Execution Officer Handbook: What Happens After a Commodity Trade Is Agreed?

*A practical guide to physical commodity contract execution, logistics, documentation, payment control, and AI-assisted post-trade operations.*

A commodity trade is agreed. The buyer and seller are satisfied. The price is fixed, the quantity is confirmed, and everyone can finally get on with their lives.

Except this is where the real work begins.

Somebody needs to prepare the contract, verify the documents, arrange transport, coordinate inspections, monitor delivery, reconcile quantities, confirm payments, and make sure nobody accidentally changes the agreement along the way.

And when something inevitably goes wrong, somebody needs to know what happened, who is responsible, and what the contract actually says.

That person is usually the **Contract Execution Officer**.

*The Contract Execution Officer Handbook: A Practical Guide to Physical Commodity Contract Execution with MN7R* was developed by the MN7R Team and published by AMI Press in 2026.

It's a 236-page practical guide to managing physical commodity transactions from commercial agreement to final operational and financial closeout.

The book focuses primarily on agricultural commodity trading, but its principles apply to a much broader range of physical commodity operations.

And importantly, although it uses the [MN7R platform](https://mn7r.com/) to demonstrate modern execution workflows, the underlying methodology is designed to work independently of any particular ERP system or software.

## **Trading creates value. Execution protects it.**

There is a persistent misconception in commodity markets that the difficult part is negotiating the deal.

Sometimes it is.

But a profitable trade can become an unprofitable transaction surprisingly quickly.

A missing inspection certificate delays payment.

An incorrect delivery instruction sends cargo to the wrong place.

Two departments work from different versions of the contract.

A shipment arrives, but the final quantity cannot be reconciled.

Someone agrees to change a delivery deadline without obtaining the necessary commercial authorization.

None of these problems requires a spectacular market crash.

A few poorly documented decisions can be enough.

The central idea behind *The Contract Execution Officer Handbook* is that **contract execution should be treated as a professional control discipline rather than an administrative function**.

The execution officer doesn't replace the trader, logistics provider, inspector, lawyer, or finance department.

Instead, the officer connects their responsibilities into one controlled operational process.

Every obligation needs an owner. Every important fact needs supporting evidence. Every material decision needs appropriate authority.

And every contract should be understandable long after the people who executed it have moved on to something else.

## **From trade agreement to contract closeout**

The handbook follows the complete lifecycle of a physical commodity contract.

It begins with the transition from trading to execution.

This is a particularly important moment because a completed commercial negotiation doesn't necessarily contain all the information required for successful execution.

The execution team must understand the agreed commercial terms, identify unresolved issues, verify counterparties, establish documentary requirements, and determine which actions must happen first.

From there, the book moves through contract preparation, amendments, operational readiness, transportation, inspection, reconciliation, and payment.

It covers practical situations involving road and rail transport, port operations, sea freight, bills of lading, inspection certificates, quantity discrepancies, and financial settlement.

The final stages address billing, brokerage commissions, claims, and contract closeout.

One recurring principle is that **completing a task is not the same as completing an obligation**.

Sending an invoice doesn't mean payment has been received.

A vessel arriving at the destination doesn't necessarily mean delivery obligations have been fulfilled.

And a contract shouldn't be marked closed merely because nobody has sent another email about it for three weeks.

Progress needs evidence.

## **Evidence, authority, and the problem with optimistic status reports**

One of the most useful ideas in the handbook is the distinction between operational status and operational evidence.

Consider a shipment marked as delivered.

What exactly does that mean?

Did the transport company report arrival?

Did the consignee accept the goods?

Was the delivered quantity confirmed?

Were the relevant documents signed?

Was the cargo accepted without reservations?

These are different questions.

A status label describes what someone believes happened.

Evidence establishes what can actually be demonstrated.

The same principle applies to contractual authority.

An execution officer may coordinate an amendment, but that doesn't necessarily authorize them to approve it.

An AI assistant may identify a discrepancy, but that doesn't give it authority to change the commercial position.

The handbook consistently separates **facts, interpretations, responsibilities, and decisions**.

This approach makes execution more predictable and significantly easier to audit.

## **A practical reference, not another collection of abstract principles**

The book contains 25 chapters organized into five main parts, covering execution fundamentals, contract formation, physical performance, operational exceptions, and working with MN7R.

It also includes substantial practical reference material.

Readers will find operational checklists for commercial handovers, contract reviews, transport monitoring, payment control, exception management, and closeout.

There are communication templates for requesting missing documents, notifying counterparties about delays, escalating issues, confirming operational status, and following up on payments.

The handbook also explains how to build contract-specific execution checklists rather than blindly copying generic templates from one transaction to another.

Finally, it includes practical scenarios with questions and suggested approaches, along with exercises using the MN7R demonstration environment.

The intention is straightforward: help execution officers develop a repeatable way of thinking about contracts, especially when circumstances become complicated.

## **MN7R: Turning execution principles into operational software**

I've been working on the [MN7R](https://mn7r.com/) ecosystem as part of a broader effort to build better infrastructure for physical commodity brokerage and execution.

One recurring problem is that operational information tends to become fragmented.

The contract is in one system.

Email correspondence is somewhere else.

Transport documentation lives in shared folders.

Payment information sits inside accounting software.

Someone maintains a separate spreadsheet.

And the person who actually understands what is happening is probably on holiday.

MN7R approaches this problem by organizing execution around a **Contract Dossier**.

Instead of treating contracts as static documents, the platform connects commercial information, operational responsibilities, documents, approvals, milestones, and financial status to the same transaction.

The goal is to maintain one coherent operational record from trade handover through closeout.

But software alone isn't enough.

The system needs clear rules about what constitutes completion, which information is authoritative, when escalation is required, and who can approve material decisions.

That is why the handbook explains the operational methodology first and introduces MN7R later.

The book also distinguishes the platform's intended operating model from its verified released capabilities. Technology evolves; professional execution standards should remain stable.

## **Where AI belongs in commodity contract execution**

Artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly useful in commodity operations.

It can analyze contracts, identify missing information, summarize correspondence, retrieve historical records, highlight operational risks, and help prepare routine documentation.

But commodity execution is a particularly poor environment for an AI system that confidently invents answers.

An incorrect interpretation of a contractual clause can have financial consequences.

A missed documentary requirement can delay cargo.

And an AI-generated recommendation is not evidence that something actually happened.

The handbook therefore describes AI as a supervised professional assistant.

Within MN7R, the AI Officer Assistant is intended to reduce repetitive analytical work while keeping consequential decisions under human control.

**AI analyzes. Professionals verify and decide.**

This distinction is important as more companies begin introducing agentic workflows into logistics, brokerage, and commodity trading.

The objective shouldn't be to automate every possible action.

It should be to make operational decisions faster, clearer, and more defensible.

## **Who should read The Contract Execution Officer Handbook?**

The book is primarily intended for professionals involved in physical commodity trading and post-trade operations.

That includes contract execution officers, commodity brokers, trading operations teams, logistics coordinators, and professionals responsible for documentation, inspections, settlement, and contract administration.

It can also serve as a reference for managers designing execution processes, organizations introducing commodity trading software, and teams exploring AI-assisted operational workflows.

For people entering commodity execution, it provides a structured introduction to the profession.

For experienced practitioners, it offers a way to review existing procedures, identify weaknesses, standardize controls, and improve operational visibility.

The book is deliberately practical. It isn't intended to replace legal advice, contractual rules, or professional judgment.

## **Better execution begins with better questions**

My main interest in developing systems such as MN7R isn't simply replacing spreadsheets with nicer interfaces or adding AI to another enterprise application.

It's making complex operational work more understandable, reliable, and easier to supervise.

And that starts with a surprisingly small set of questions.

What do we know?

What evidence supports it?

What remains uncertain?

Who owns the next action?

Who has the authority to decide?

What will prove that the obligation has been completed?

Those questions apply whether an organization manages five contracts or five thousand.

They also remain relevant whether the underlying system is a spreadsheet, a conventional ERP, or an AI-assisted execution platform.

The technology will change.

The need for reliable evidence, clear ownership, and accountable decisions won't.

**The Contract Execution Officer Handbook** is an attempt to bring those principles together into one practical professional reference.

Because a trade isn't successful simply because someone agreed to it.

It's successful when the agreement has actually been executed, the obligations are satisfied, the money is accounted for, and the entire process can be reconstructed from evidence.

That's the part worth getting right.
