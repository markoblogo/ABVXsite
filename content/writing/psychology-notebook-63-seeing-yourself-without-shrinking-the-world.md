_A thematic selection of translated notebook fragments._

_Cover image supplied from the owner-authorized Psychology Notebook reserve._

Our shadow is the part of ourselves we have expelled and prefer not to encounter, lest it destroy our bright self-image. Tell me what is in your shadow, and I will tell you who you are.

---

It is interesting that putting yourself down—a toxic inner critic constantly handing out blows—does not mean that you assess yourself critically. In fact, this self-bullying can coexist with a complete inability to receive objective criticism from outside:

‘What do you mean, I did it wrong? Yes, I know I am bad!’

---

Being relentlessly positive, reaching for the light and supporting everything good against everything bad can turn someone into a superficial, boring character in an instant. What matters is not the absence or denial of abysses, darkness and shadows, but how a person deals with them.

---

A lack of self-respect and a wound to one's sense of dignity mean that even a hint of disrespect can cause pain and rage.

---

Being fixated on yourself and being interested in yourself are different processes. Self-fixation often follows a weak sense of self, when the constant ‘me, me, me’ is a way of noticing and hearing yourself at all. It can also follow emotional pain, which makes a person self-centred and unable to notice the world around them. Being interested in yourself means being able to notice yourself and act from a sense of dignity rather than pain, while still noticing other people and the world, and relating your interests to theirs.

---

A weak sense of self means that, on the one hand, you constantly need confirmation of yourself; on the other, there is nobody inside to claim that confirmation.

---

The need for other people's love is understandable in childhood. A child without love has less chance of surviving.

For adults, what matters more is not universal love, but the ability to influence and change the world.

---

Sometimes what matters is not figuring yourself out, but stepping away from self-examination and turning towards other people and the world around you.

---

On closer inspection, the idea of being deprived—that life favours other people but never poor little you—turns out to be a way of feeling unique, and an inability to feel gratitude when life does offer something. Instead of ‘thank you’, the magic word is ‘not enough’.

---

The need for endless admiration and confirmation of one's exceptional nature often cannot be satisfied when an inner sense of worthlessness multiplies every bit of external admiration by zero.
