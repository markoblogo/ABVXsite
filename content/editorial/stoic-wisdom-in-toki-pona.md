# **Stoic Wisdom in Toki Pona**

The first book I ever translated into toki pona was **Marcus Aurelius’ *Meditations***.

It was also one of the first books I published.

At the time, I did not know whether there was any meaningful audience for classical philosophy translated into a minimalist constructed language. It was partly a publishing experiment and partly a personal one: I wanted to see what would happen to a text such as *Meditations* when its ideas were forced through the extremely small vocabulary of toki pona.

The experiment worked better than I expected.

*Meditations in Toki Pona* became one of the best-selling books in my entire publishing catalogue, and it remains one of the strongest titles at BWA Express.

That result convinced me that this was not just an isolated curiosity.

There was a real overlap between people interested in toki pona and people interested in philosophy, particularly practical philosophy: texts about attention, self-control, mortality, responsibility, judgement and how to live.

So one book gradually became a series.

## **Why Stoicism works in toki pona**

There is an interesting compatibility between Stoic writing and toki pona.

Toki pona has a very small vocabulary. It deliberately avoids the enormous conceptual precision available in languages such as English, French or Latin.

At first that seems like a disadvantage.

Philosophy depends on distinctions. Stoic texts contain ideas about virtue, judgement, nature, reason, control, desire, duty and the relationship between the individual and the world. Translating all of that into a language with only a tiny number of basic concepts inevitably removes distinctions.

But that limitation can also become useful.

When you cannot rely on specialised terminology, you have to ask what a sentence is actually saying.

What is under my control?

What is not?

What do I need?

What am I afraid of?

What is a good action?

What happens if I remove the label I have attached to something?

These are already very close to the way toki pona tends to deconstruct ideas.

A complex philosophical expression has to be rebuilt from simpler concepts.

For me, that makes toki pona surprisingly well suited to **slow philosophical reading**.

## **Marcus Aurelius**

The starting point remains **Marcus Aurelius’ *Meditations***.

The original work is already unusual. It was not written as a philosophical textbook or even primarily for publication. Marcus was writing reminders to himself.

That gives much of the book a compressed, repetitive and practical character.

Remember that you will die.

Do not waste time judging other people.

Distinguish between the event and your opinion about the event.

Do the work in front of you.

Accept what you cannot change.

This kind of writing survives radical linguistic compression remarkably well.

In some places, translating it into toki pona makes the thought less precise. In others, stripping away the terminology makes the underlying instruction almost more visible.

That tension became one of the reasons I wanted to continue the project.

## **Epictetus**

The series naturally expanded to **Epictetus and the *Enchiridion***.

If Marcus Aurelius often feels like somebody reminding himself how to behave, Epictetus can feel more like somebody constructing a practical operating system.

His central distinction between what depends on us and what does not is almost ideally suited to toki pona.

The vocabulary becomes smaller, but the decision becomes sharper.

Is this mine to control?

Or is it part of the world outside me?

Again, the point of the translation is not to replace a scholarly edition of Epictetus. A minimalist language cannot preserve every philosophical nuance of the Greek text.

Instead, it creates another way of inspecting the structure of the idea.

## **Seneca**

**Seneca’s *On the Shortness of Life*** became another book in the line.

It is one of those ancient texts that keeps feeling contemporary because its central complaint has barely changed.

We say that life is short while simultaneously giving enormous amounts of it away.

To meetings.

To status.

To distraction.

To other people’s priorities.

To things we do not actually care about.

This is another reason Stoic and adjacent classical texts work well in toki pona. Many of their strongest ideas do not depend on elaborate terminology. They depend on seeing ordinary behaviour differently.

A tiny language can make that directness difficult to avoid.

## **Cicero and the wider classical tradition**

The series also includes **Cicero**.

I use the title *Stoic Wisdom* somewhat broadly rather than as a strict academic classification of every author in the collection.

What interests me is the larger classical conversation around ethics, practical philosophy, mortality, responsibility and the good life.

Cicero belongs naturally in that conversation, even when his philosophical position cannot simply be reduced to Stoicism.

The publishing line is therefore curated around a way of reading rather than around maintaining a perfectly closed philosophical taxonomy.

## **A free Reader’s Kit**

As with many of my publishing series, I wanted there to be a free point of entry.

The result is **The Toki Pona Reader’s Kit**, built around the **Golden Verses of Pythagoras**.

It is available as a free book and is intended not only as a philosophical text but also as a reading and learning resource.

That combination is important to me.

Someone may arrive because they are interested in philosophy and begin learning toki pona through the book.

Someone else may already know toki pona and discover a classical text they had never considered reading.

The free book removes the need to decide in advance whether the experiment is worth paying for.

You can simply begin reading.

## **Beyond the books**

The series also has its own dedicated landing site.

And, appropriately for a publishing project built around an experimental language, the site itself became another experiment.

It can be read in conventional English, but also in **toki pona**, **sitelen pona** and **emoji pona**.

Those versions are generated through the same translation and rendering tools I have developed for my wider toki pona publishing ecosystem.

So the series gradually became more than five books.

It became a small demonstration of how a publishing project can exist across several layers:

the original philosophical text,

the toki pona translation,

the visual sitelen pona representation,

the book,

the website,

and the software that connects them.

## **Philosophy as something to inspect**

I do not see these editions as substitutes for conventional translations.

If you want to study Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus or Seneca seriously, you should absolutely read good translations in larger languages, compare editions and look at the historical and philosophical context.

Toki pona does something different.

It removes options.

And because it removes options, both the translator and the reader have to inspect the idea more carefully.

What is essential here?

What can disappear without destroying the thought?

Which distinction matters?

Which distinction exists only because our normal language gives us a word for it?

That is what interests me about **Stoic Wisdom in Toki Pona**.

It began with one experimental translation of Marcus Aurelius.

That book found an audience, became one of the strongest titles in my catalogue, and grew into a small collection of classical philosophy, free learning material and multilingual publishing infrastructure.

A tiny language turned out to be a surprisingly useful place to think about very old questions.
