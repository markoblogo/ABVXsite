# **Chinese Wisdom in Toki Pona**

This series started from a simple observation.

Some of my most successful toki pona books were philosophical texts. Readers were buying Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca and other works from the Greco-Roman tradition, and it became clear that there was a natural overlap between people interested in toki pona and people interested in philosophy.

That made me wonder what would happen if I applied the same approach to classical Chinese thought.

In some ways, the connection felt even more natural.

Toki pona is a deliberately minimal language. It reduces a large amount of meaning to a very small vocabulary and forces the reader to slow down, interpret and reconstruct ideas rather than simply consume them.

Classical Chinese philosophical texts often reward exactly that kind of reading.

So I created a small series of Chinese philosophical works translated into toki pona, with a particular emphasis on visual and parallel reading through **sitelen pona**.

## **Dao De Jing**

The first book was the **Dao De Jing**.

I did not want it to feel like a conventional translated paperback. I treated it more as an art book and a reading object.

Each spread places the original Chinese passage on the left, presented in Chinese calligraphy. On the right, the same passage appears prominently in **sitelen pona**, accompanied by a smaller toki pona text.

The point is not only translation.

The three forms create three different ways of looking at the same idea.

The Chinese text preserves the visual and historical source.

Toki pona compresses the meaning into a radically limited vocabulary.

Sitelen pona makes that compression visible.

This format naturally slows the reader down. Instead of moving quickly through an English explanation of Laozi, you compare structures, symbols and meanings and spend more time with a very small amount of text.

The book found an audience, which encouraged me to continue the experiment.

## **The Art of War**

The second major work in the series became **Sun Tzu's The Art of War** in toki pona.

At first, a military strategy text might seem like an unusual companion to the Dao De Jing. But *The Art of War* is also a book built around compression.

Its ideas are concise, structural and often expressed through oppositions: strength and weakness, visibility and concealment, action and restraint, direct and indirect approaches.

That makes it particularly interesting to translate into a language where every distinction has to earn its place.

A toki pona translation cannot preserve every lexical distinction available in a larger language. It has to identify the structure underneath the sentence.

For me, that is one of the most interesting things about translating philosophy into toki pona in the first place.

Translation becomes interpretation.

## **Universal Love**

The third book is **Universal Love**, based on the Mohist tradition and the thought of **Mozi**.

Unlike the first two books, I made this one freely available.

I try to include at least one free book in many of my publishing lines, particularly when the series involves an unfamiliar language or an unusual reading format.

It gives people a way to try the idea before buying anything.

It also fits the subject particularly well. A philosophical argument about impartial care and extending concern beyond one's immediate circle seems like a good text to make openly accessible.

So the Chinese Wisdom line currently consists of three core books:

**Dao De Jing**, **The Art of War**, and **Universal Love**.

It is deliberately small.

## **The Heart Sutra**

Later, I also translated the **Heart Sutra** into toki pona.

Strictly speaking, I do not treat it as part of the Chinese philosophy series. It belongs to a different religious and philosophical tradition, and its history extends far beyond China.

But it feels closely related to the same experiment.

I have had a long personal interest in Zen Buddhism, and the Heart Sutra is perhaps one of the clearest examples of a text in which very few words are expected to carry an enormous conceptual weight.

That makes toki pona an unusually interesting medium for it.

The translation is available separately and, like *Universal Love*, is free through my collection of free toki pona books.

## **Philosophy through compression**

What interests me most in this project is not the novelty of seeing famous texts in an unusual language.

It is what minimal language does to philosophy.

A translation into a large modern language can often hide interpretation behind precision. There are enough words available to reproduce distinctions, terminology and explanatory nuance.

Toki pona removes much of that safety.

Its vocabulary is tiny.

The translator has to decide what an idea is actually doing.

The reader then has to decide how much meaning to reconstruct from that compressed form.

With sitelen pona, another layer appears: ideas are not only compressed linguistically, but also represented visually.

That is why I think of these books less as replacements for conventional translations and more as tools for **slow reading**.

You can read the original.

You can read a conventional translation.

Then you can look at the toki pona version and ask:

What remained when most of the vocabulary disappeared?

What had to be interpreted?

What became clearer?

And what became impossible to say directly?

That is the real purpose of **Chinese Wisdom in Toki Pona**.

It is a small publishing experiment in philosophy, translation and compression, using some of the oldest texts in the world through one of the smallest modern languages.
